# Literature gap study: thermal modelling and coolant, material behaviour, mill dynamics and gauge / tension control

Scope: thin-gauge aluminium cold rolling (foil 6-60 um; sheet from about 1 mm down), with the band-diagnosis part of `physicroll` as the reference implementation. Web research only; the repository was read, not changed. It builds on `process-temperatures.md` (sources `T1`-`T39`) and `battery-foil-grades-by-gauge.md` (sources `G1`-`G44`) and does not repeat them.

**Read tags.** [FT] full text read; [EX] excerpt (search-tool extract or part of the text); [AB] abstract or snippet only; [VP] vendor or trade page; [CIT] seen only as a citation in another source. "Computed here" = my own arithmetic with textbook constants (steel k = 43 W/mK, rho*c = 3.6e6 J/m3K, a = 1.2e-5 m2/s; aluminium k = 230 W/mK, rho*c = 2.43e6 J/m3K, alpha = 23.6e-6 /K, E = 70 GPa); these are checks, not literature values. "From memory, not verified" marks anything not found in a source during this pass.

**Effort scale.** S = up to about two days inside the existing module structure; M = one to two weeks, new sub-model plus tests; L = more than two weeks, or needs a new solver or plant data before it means anything.

**Band types.** LW-fixed = lengthwise band at a fixed width position; LW-short = short-lived lengthwise band; TR = transverse mark.

## 2. State of the art (concise, sourced)

### 2.A Thermal modelling and coolant

**A-i. Roll and strip thermal models.** The standard formulation since Tseng is a coupled roll-strip problem: a rotating roll with a thin cyclic surface layer over a slowly varying core, a strip with plastic heat in the volume and friction heat at the interface, and a thermal contact conductance between them [LA1 AB; LA2 AB; LA3 AB; LA4 AB]. Tseng's finite-difference model was built for "very high velocity, high temperature variation in a very thin layer, curved boundary, and bimaterial interface" and covers cold and hot aluminium rolling [LA1; LA2]. Part 2 of his interface review recommends the contact-conductance approach over a prescribed partition [LA3]. Patula's analytic solution shows that the effect of per-revolution heating and cooling penetrates "usually less than 4 percent of the radius", that the bulk can be computed without internal gradients, and that this bulk approach is "not suited for determining accurate temperatures at the roll surface" [LA6 AB]. Tseng's skin-layer rule (depth where the cyclic swing is below 1 %, coefficient 7 at Peclet number 1e5) is quoted in two UBC theses; the formula itself was lost in the extraction [LA7 EX]. For aluminium cold mills specifically: a coupled work-roll / strip model with deformation and friction heat, boundary conditions and 2-D alternating-direction differences [LA20 AB; full text T18]; a five-region roll boundary (exit contact, bite, spray, air, backup-roll contact) [LA26 AB]; and a comparison of a "phase shift" (time-resolved periodic load) with a circumferential-average method, the first more accurate and the second faster [LA27 AB]. The project's model is of the circumferential-average, axisymmetric class used for on-line thermal crown in hot mills (2-D axisymmetric implicit FV with parameters identified from measured crown, errors within 10 um [LA37 EX]; ADI for on-line use [LA37 AB]). Guo's and Ginzburg's models were found only as citations (section 7).

**A-ii. Bite heat partition and contact conductance.** Measured: foil mill, strip 0.11-0.33 and rolls 0.67-0.89, the roll share rising with cooling [LA19 AB, re-read; still abstract only]. Hot rolling of AA5xxx on a pilot mill gave 200-450 kW/m2K [LA21 AB]; the thesis table spans 65-464 kW/m2K from bulk temperatures and 60-1200 from surface thermocouples, and it distributes friction heat by a diffusivity ratio [LA21 EX]. Cold rolling of aluminium at 68 and 168 mm/s: the interface thermal resistance "varies linearly along the arc of contact" and is "sensitive to rolling speed" [LA22 AB]. Aluminium models use 25-40 kW/m2K while measurements span 7-800 [T34]. A rough-surface statistical conductance with a Johnson-Cook flow stress has been used for cold strip rolling [LA23 AB].
*Computed here (a check, not a source):* at foil gauge the strip is thermally thin (Fourier number about 100 for a 24 um mean gauge and 0.2 ms contact). With perfect contact the roll skin takes heat in proportion to `(2/sqrt(pi))*rho*c*sqrt(a*t_c)` = about 200 J/m2K per roll against about 29 J/m2K for half the foil, i.e. about 0.87 to the rolls, inside the measured 0.67-0.89. An oil film of 70 nm (the lengthwise demo's value) conducts about 1.9 MW/m2K (k_oil about 0.13 W/mK, from memory), far above 25-40 kW/m2K, so at foil gauge the interface is close to thermally perfect. The same estimate at 0.25 mm and 0.4 ms gives roughly half to the rolls, not 0.16. At very high speed the thicker film (h_s proportional to speed) and the shorter contact both cut the roll share, which is the direction of Kerth's "almost adiabatic" statement [T4]. The assumed contact lengths (2 mm and 5 mm) carry most of the uncertainty.

**A-iii. Spray heat-transfer coefficients.** No absolute measured coefficient for a kerosene-type coolant on a rotating work roll was found in this pass either. What exists:
- Measured, relative: Novelis (former Alcan) used a heated instrumented aluminium block and a heated rotating roll rig; base oils differ by "up to 25 % reduction in htc compared to normal paraffin"; the significant factors are spray coverage area, flow rate and roll speed; cooling is "not monotonically related to the flow rate" with level control; target overlap 10-50 % [LA15 EX, slide text; T37]. HTC of hydrocarbon base oils rises with n-paraffin content and can be estimated from composition [LA16 AB].
- Measured, water on a rotating roll: 10-22 kW/m2K **averaged over the sprayed area**, independent of pressure and about linear in flow [LA17 AB]; coefficient distributions against roll speed 0.1-10 m/s and surface temperature, saturation when the roll is flooded [LA18 AB].
- Measured, oil against water on a plate: oil 3-6 times lower [T19].
- Simulated for aluminium mills: 2.5-4.3 kW/m2K [T18]; coefficient falls as the roll surface cools, rises with jet speed and with colder coolant, "verified by experiments" (numbers not in the abstract) [LA19 AB]; ranking of factors: coolant viscosity, jet speed, coolant temperature, spray angle [LA19 AB].
- Hot mill, water / emulsion: 5.5-30 kW/m2K (Parke and Baker, as quoted), 17.5-22 kW/m2K fitted to measured thermal cambers; the same thesis tests the "fast" camber model with Poisson's ratio 0.33 and 0 [LA8 EX].
- Overall coefficient in a design tool: 4.65-5.5 kW/m2K, coolant not named [LA29 FT].
The published values are on different bases (jet zone, sprayed area, overall). Sprayed-area water values divided by 3-6 give about 1.7-7 kW/m2K on the sprayed area (computed here); a circumferential mean is that times the wetted fraction of the circumference.

**A-iv. Thermal crown dynamics and time constants.** Alcan's 1975 paper on thin, hard aluminium describes "three separate thermal effects ... each with a different time constant": the short-term transient "does not affect the thermal crown directly", the medium-term one is used for local shape and the long-term one for overall shape [LA9 AB; values not in the abstract]. The plant-identified model the project cites is from an aluminium producer (Sumitomo Light Metal): the coolant is the local-zone actuator, "although [its] response is lower than the response of mechanical actuator" [LA10 AB; the 20 s is not in the abstract]; the same group built foil-mill flatness models "based on results from an experimental study in an actual plant" with "detection time delay and mutual interaction between the actuators" [LA11 AB]. A vendor paper simulates a zone sprayed with 83 C oil on a 125 mm roll otherwise at 60 C: the diameter change is plotted on a 0-4 um axis against 0-100 s of spray time [LA12 EX; curve not extractable]. In words: after a spray is turned down "the initial change is quite narrow and relatively fast on the roll but over time it will grow in magnitude and area until a wave or buckle appears" [LA13 EX]. A foil coil takes "as long as four hours" to roll, so the roll thermal curve has to be predicted for set-up [LA36 EX]. On/off valve control brings "thermal lobes, hydraulic hammers, and thermal waves" [LA38 AB, hot mill].
*Computed here:* the surface of a half-space answers a step in the coefficient h on the time scale `t* = k*rho*c/h^2`: 25 s at 2.5, 13 s at 3.4, 8 s at 4.3 kW/m2K (57 % of the surface-temperature change at t = t*; textbook solution, from memory). This is the "medium" scale and it is the scale of surface temperature, not of radius.

**A-v. Strip temperature across the width, and after cooling.** "Any gradient in transverse temperature across the strip will change / degrade the shape as a finished coil cools"; without monitoring it is "undetected during rolling"; shape logs show good shape while the coil shape deteriorates after cooling [LA13 EX]. For steel, 1 C across the width is equated to 2.5 MPa or 1.2 I-units, and the flatness target curve is offset by a fitted temperature polynomial [LA30 EX]. For aluminium the same arithmetic gives 2.4 I-units and 1.65 MPa per kelvin (computed here). No source models a width-wise change of flow stress or oil film from temperature.

**A-vi. Coil temperature and coil cooling.** Coil models are 2-D or 3-D conduction with an equivalent radial conductivity that depends on winding pressure, roughness and the gas or oil gap [LA33 EX; LA32 EX; LA34 AB]; boundary coefficients are identified from pyrometer measurements [LA32]; a gradient between surface and core remains after 40 h in steel coils [LA34]. A through-process study winds 0.28 mm aluminium at 140 C and 30 MPa: a crowned profile builds up to 80 MPa hoop stress at the centre of the outer wraps, creep during five days of cooling turns this into -20 MPa, and the strip shows new centre pockets after unwinding [LA31 EX]. Battery-foil coil limits and property decay are in T1, T2.

**A-vii. Viscosity, temperature and friction.** Viscosity falls about 1.5-1.7 % per kelvin [T12; docs 8.1]; the inlet film is proportional to viscosity [docs 8.1]. A mixed-lubrication foil model gives load, film and contact area against speed for 50 to 25 um [LC25 AB]; a Stribeck-type curve for foil rolling shows friction falling with speed to a minimum and then rising [LC25 AB]. In closed-gap rolling a thicker film gives thinner foil [T4].

**A-viii. Temperature and local flow stress.** 1100-H18: 165 / 150 MPa at room temperature, 145 / 130 at 100 C, 125 at 150 C (long holding) [T31, T39]; 99.5 % Al at 200 C about 35 % below room temperature [T4]. That is about -0.15 to -0.3 % per kelvin (computed here). Plant-rolled material lies below laboratory-rolled material because of deformation heating; the difference is in stage III, the terminal stage-IV rate is about the same [LB4 EX, for 5182].

**A-ix. Fire and oil mist.** Insurer guidance: kerosene-type rolling fluid is an ignitable liquid, often of low flash point; the spray "likely produces an ignition sensitive, flammable atmosphere"; exhaust ventilation is required "to control flammable mist and vapor", with CO2 at the bite, total flooding and deluge [LA35 EX]. One US rule caps oil temperature (155 F appears in the retrieved snippet) and sets a minimum initial boiling point of 380 F [LA35 AB; T13 FT]. A foil-mill fire at 210 to 130 um was traced to static charge on an oil pan, with oil at 38-40 C and 600-800 L/min [LA35 FT]. These are limits on oil temperature, strip temperature and speed, not physics to be simulated.

### 2.B Material behaviour and metallurgy

**B-i. Hardening at very large strain.** AA1200 cold rolled 40-99 % (true strain 0.5-5.0): neither the boundary spacing nor the flow stress saturates, also when Taylor-factor changes are considered [LB3 AB]. Stage IV has a low hardening rate, "usually below 100 MPa" at ambient temperature; foil rolling reaches strains of six to seven and "the strength attained relies on maintaining a level of work hardening even at high strains" [LB4 EX]. Stress saturation is reached in super-purity aluminium at room temperature and in commercial purity above about 100 C [LB5 EX]. Stage-IV forms in use: Tome's modified Voce `sigma = (sigma_v + theta_IV*eps)*(1 - exp(-theta_0*eps/sigma_v))`, and the Kocks-Mecking, Nes and Holmedal models [LB4].

**B-ii. Work softening.** An Al-0.69 mol% Fe alloy softens beyond 80 % reduction (hardness peak at 60 %), while A1050 peaks at 80 % and settles near 43 HV; the cause is a matrix purified to about 99.98 % because Si is taken up by Al13Fe4; 99.98-99.999 % Al behaves the same; softening is "frequently observed" above equivalent strain 2.66 [LB2 EX, most of the text]. AA8079: the starting temperature of cold rolling (30 against 70 C) changes the final strength visibly, temperature is "a crucial factor for the extent of dynamic recovery", and splitting the same reduction into more passes has no noticeable effect [LB7 AB]. 8021 pouch foil: controlling coil and oil temperature avoids softening [T36]. Strength and elongation at 50 um rise with Fe from 1050 to 8021; Fe in solution as rolled is 405-469 ppm without intermediate anneal; softening starts at 200-220 C for 8079 / 8021 and above 260 C for 1050 [LB6 EX]. Cu-bearing 1100-type and Mn / Fe-in-solution grades keep hardening to strain 4-5 [G8, G26].

**B-iii. Rate and temperature sensitivity.** 1235-H18 foil, 16 um, micro-tensile tests from 1e-5 to 1e-2 /s: the stress at 2 % strain rises from about 160 to 200 MPa and the fitted rate sensitivity is m about 0.027; Lankford ratios 0.2 / 1.5 / 1.1 (rolling / diagonal / transverse) do not change with rate; the transverse stress is about 10 % above the other two; grain size about 800 nm through the thickness; a Yld2000-2d viscoplastic model is fitted [LB1 EX]. 1050-H14: m about 0.04 at 100 C and 0.07 at 200 C [LB10 EX]. Below about 100 C the hardening rate is "relatively insensitive to strain rate ... between 1e-4 and 1e2 /s" [LB4]. Split-Hopkinson data for 1100 at 300-2000 /s and 30-550 C exist [LB11 AB], as does a rate-history model for AA1050 at high rates [LB11 AB]. *Computed here:* a foil pass at 10 m/s with a 2 mm arc runs at about 2.6e3 /s; if m = 0.027 held up to that rate the flow stress would be about 1.5 times its 1e-3 /s value. The measured range ends at 1e-2 /s, so this is an extrapolation over five decades.

**B-iv. Anisotropy and texture.** Cold-rolled aluminium carries the beta-fibre (Brass, S, Copper); solute and shear banding shift it toward Goss and Brass [LB5 EX]; 1235 after 90 % cold rolling shows a copper-type texture with a maximum orientation density of 18 [LB14 EX]. The 16 um foil numbers are in B-iii.

**B-v. Particles and pinholes.** In an 8xxx foil alloy the mean Fe-particle diameter falls from 2.5 to 0.65 um through to ultra-light gauge and the particle statistics correlate with pinhole density [LB16 AB]. In 40 um 8021 the 2-3 um Al-Fe particles do not perforate; foreign particles of 10-70 um do (melt inclusions, filter-aid particles from the rolling oil, dust); the interface opens above 38 % cumulative reduction, in the forward-slip zone [LB15 EX]. Coarse Al-Fe-O compounds, Cr-bearing intermetallics and alpha phase above 2 um are named for 8079 and 8011 [LB16 AB]. Pinhole limits by gauge are in G3.

**B-vi. Recovery in the coil.** T1, T2 (elongation decay above 90 C), T32 (kinetics at 140-220 C), G26 (strength after 100 / 150 / 200 C). 8021 foil at 40 um: 47.6 HV as rolled, stable to 180 C at 40 C/h and softening from the start at 10 C/h [LB18 EX].

### 2.C Mill dynamics, vibration, gauge and tension control

**C-i. Third-octave chatter.** Four mechanisms are distinguished: negative damping (Tamiya; Tlusty), mode coupling (Yun), model matching and regeneration between stands [LC1 AB; LC5 EX; LC4 EX]. Yun's dynamic bite model gives gauge, speeds, tensions, force and torque as functions of the roll gap **and its rate** [LC1 AB]; Hu, Zhao and Ehmann combine a homogeneous process model with structure models and give stability criteria per mechanism [LC2 AB]; Zhao and Ehmann add inter-stand tension and transport delay and explain "the existence of an optimum friction condition" [LC3 AB]. The direct negative-damping effect of friction is robust across structural models, while the indirect effect through the shift of the chatter frequency is model-dependent, so 1-DOF structures "should be used with caution" [LC5 EX]. Span dynamics: wave propagation changes critical speed and frequency slightly (experiment 5.81 m/s, 124.6 Hz; 6.42 m/s with wave dynamics, 6.57 without) and matters more for fifth-octave frequencies [LC7 EX]. Unsteady lubrication: with a limiting shear stress the lubricant viscosity has little effect and the limiting stress a large one; single-stand simulations over-predict two-stand critical speeds by 42 % on average because regeneration is missing [LC6 EX]; a film-dependent friction law inside the dynamic bite reproduces the negative damping [LC10 EX; LC10 AB for an aluminium mill]. Coupled vertical-torsional-horizontal models put the stability boundary on three modal lines [LC9 EX, AB]. Strip transverse vibration with feed motion gives a stand frequency that differs from the natural frequency (120-190 Hz range, 140 Hz second mode of a sixth stand) [LC11 EX]. Aluminium: third-octave resonances "commonly ... between 100 and 150 Hz, but sometimes as high as 300 Hz", growth in less than a second, above the bandwidth of gauge systems so that the gauge shows only an average [LC14 FT]; a four-stand AA3104 mill at 0.15 mm, designed for 1800 m/min, held at 1000 m/min by chatter [LC15 AB]. No chatter study on a foil mill was found.

**C-ii. Fifth octave and marks.** 500-700 Hz [LC16; LC9] or 600-1200 Hz [LC13 EX; LC14 FT]; no measurable gauge variation; marks develop on the backup-roll barrel and print through with 10-40 mm spacing; always a forced source (gear teeth, bearings, couplings, or "periodic sub-micron features on the roll surface produced during roll grinding") [LC14 FT]. One measured case: 1190 Hz, 12 mm pitch, gear mesh at 355 Hz [LC13 EX]. An aluminium case: 315 Hz at 470 m/min, proportional to speed, chock displacement about 0.6 um, a work-roll bearing inner race [LC14 FT; already the project's bearing demo]. Chatter-mark bands on cluster mills appear without thickness change [LC12 EX].

**C-iii. Gauge control at foil gauge.** "Speed-tension optimization is used in foil mill applications, since the strip thickness depends very heavily on tension and rolling speed"; speed is raised until tension reaches its control limit or speed its technological limit [LC18 VP, technical brochure]. A foil AGC design uses a Smith predictor and variable gain against the transport delay, decoupling, a tension-adaptive scheme "according to the tension oscillating problem", variable acceleration in speed AGC, and a main plus an optimising controller [LC19 AB]. In foil rolling the work rolls touch outside the strip, so the measured load is not the load on the foil [LA36 EX]. Sheet-gauge strategy: feedback, feed-forward, mass flow, speed feed-forward, eccentricity and tension feed-forward [LC18]; the delay to the gauge "essentially determines" the feedback dynamics at low speed [LC18]. Mass-flow control on foil stock at 0.152 to 0.128 mm is described by one vendor [LC20 VP].

**C-iv. Eccentricity and hardness.** Backup-roll eccentricity comes from grinding and assembly and is also "thermally induced during work roll changes and other stoppages"; cyclic hardness variation of 2 % "can easily occur in aluminum alloys as a result of uneven cooling", once per decoiler revolution; compensation of the first three harmonics improved the standard deviation by about 38 % at 950 um [LC18 EX]. Compensation from the exit gauge with phase correction for gauge lag and transport [LC21 EX].

**C-v. Tension dynamics.** Four causes of cyclic gauge from the reels: coil ovality and eccentricity; "a resonance condition resulting from a mass / spring system" of coil and strip span; friction or binding in the reel drives; and gauge variation left by a previous pass with the same problems [LC21 EX]. A tension regulator cannot attenuate a once-per-revolution disturbance near its own bandwidth and may amplify it [LC22 EX]. Indirect (torque) control is better at low speed, direct (measured) control at speed [LC28 EX]. Winder control is tuned from experimental frequency identification with the span taken as taut [LC23 AB].

**C-vi. Sensors.** Foil gauges: accuracy 0.1-0.2 %, response below 10 ms for an isotope foil gauge [LC24 VP]. Chatter detection: accelerometer on the upper backup roll or top housing; sampling 5.1-51 kHz; the work roll shows the chatter frequency about two seconds before divergence [LC7 EX]. Shape control computes every 0.05 s at above 30 m/s; final foil pass held within 6 I-units around a target of 30 I-units wavy edge [LA12 EX].

## 4. Ranked lists

### 4.1 By importance for the plant's band problem (lengthwise bands at fixed width positions, continuous or short-lived, and transverse marks, on battery foil 50 um to 12-19 um)

1. **A1** strip temperature across the width as a flatness term. Same order as the reporting threshold per kelvin, cheap, and it changes the in-mill against after-release verdicts the tool is built around.
2. **A2** local flow stress and oil film against local temperature (the deferred item). Three effects of unknown net sign; without it the tool cannot say whether a hot stripe gives a thin or a thick band.
3. **A3 with A4** computed heat partition and bite-entry surface temperature. Removes the largest free input of the stripe result and is the prerequisite for A1 and A2.
4. **A5** spray coefficient basis, flow scaling and plant calibration. The second free input; a partly blocked nozzle is a flow change.
5. **C1** foil gauge-control regime. The only route by which tension and speed disturbances become transverse gauge bands at closed gap.
6. **A8** coil temperature, coil cooling and creep. Turns profile ridges into permanent bands and links to the published coil limits.
7. **B1** rate- and temperature-dependent flow stress. Feeds A2 and decides whether the foil bite numbers are credible.
8. **C2** reel and span tension dynamics.
9. **A6** emulation of the plant identification (settles the 20 s question once A1 and A2 exist).
10. **B2** saturation and softening by alloy group.
11. **C4** fifth-octave and grinding-induced marks.

## 8. Points where the literature disagrees with, or qualifies, the project's documents

1. **Rate sensitivity "taken as zero" (docs 8.3, tagged from memory).** A measurement on 16 um 1235-H18 foil gives m about 0.027 and a 25 % rise in stress over three decades of rate [LB1]. The statement that hardening is rate-insensitive below 100 C [LB4] covers the hardening rate, not this flow-stress level effect.
2. **A positive stage-IV slope for every alloy (docs 8.3).** Right for 1200-type and solute-bearing grades [LB3, G8]; wrong in sign for purified-matrix Al-Fe alloys and high-purity grades beyond 80 % reduction [LB2], and temperature-dependent in 8079 [LB7].
3. **"No measured spray heat-transfer coefficient for kerosene-based coolant was found" (docs 7.1).** Still true for absolute values, but measured relative data exist [LA15, LA16], and the published values the tool compares with are on different bases. The earlier research note describes the 2.5-4.3 kW/m2K as a jet-zone value [T18 as summarised in `process-temperatures.md`], while `roll_thermal.py` applies its coefficient as a circumferential mean (module docstring, l.20). If the note is right the comparison in `thermal_check` mixes two bases. This needs a re-read of T18 before anything is changed.
4. **"Thicker strip keeps much more [of the heat]" and the sheet demo's strip share 0.84 (docs 8.2, tagged from memory).** No source was found for a sheet-gauge share. A perfect-contact estimate gives about half at 0.25 mm (computed here, rough). The foil value 0.22 is supported by the same estimate.
5. **The 20 s response (docs 7.1, 14).** The literature supports the project's reading that it belongs to the local, medium scale [LA9, LA13], and adds two things the docs do not list among the possible causes: the strip-temperature term in measured flatness (A1) and the surface-temperature time scale of 8-25 s (computed here). The docs' third candidate, "bite heat that depends on the roll surface temperature", is small: the foil's heat-capacity rate limits what the bite can add to the mean coefficient to the order of 0.1-0.3 kW/m2K against 2.5-4.3 (computed here for 18 um at 10 m/s on a 260 mm roll).
6. **Friction and chatter (docs 11.4, 11.5: lower friction lowers the critical speed).** Already listed as a known disagreement. The newer sources explain an optimum friction through two competing effects [LC3, LC5] and show that single-stand negative-damping models over-predict tandem critical speeds [LC6]; for a single-stand foil mill the second point does not apply.
7. **Gaugemeter AGC at foil gauge (docs 9).** The docs already restrict it to open-gap reasoning; the vendor literature is explicit that foil gauge is controlled by speed and tension [LC18], so the section should not be read for the plant's passes at all.
